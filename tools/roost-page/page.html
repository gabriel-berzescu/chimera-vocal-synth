A jackdaw roost, a winter morning, 82 seconds

Roost Vote

Jackdaws sleep together in their hundreds and leave together in the morning. They decide when by calling. The calling builds, and when it is loud enough the whole roost is in the air within five seconds. This is that morning, made from nothing but simulated birds.

One bird, one call

The call is the jackdaw's contact call, the short tchak a jackdaw gives to keep in touch with the others. Each one here is computed from an airway: air pressed from an air sac, a syrinx with a pair of vibrating labia on each side, nine centimetres of trachea, a throat, and a beak that snaps open. No recording of a jackdaw is involved anywhere.

Real calls come in two forms. Most stay clear from start to end. A minority start clear and turn rough: about one in seven of the calls in the study, by my count from its numbers.

What is true here, and what I made up

From the birds
  • The call's length, its pitch, its falling shape and its two forms.
  • The vote. In the field, the timing of a roost's departure followed the calling: a steeper rise meant an earlier exit, and playing roost calls to a roost made it leave sooner.
  • The speed of leaving. In a mass departure every bird is in the air within about five seconds. Here it takes 4.3.
  • That birds differ. A classifier can tell real jackdaws apart by their calls about 86 times in 100, so each bird here has its own body size, its own throat and its own version of the call.
From me
  • How the vote works inside. Each bird has a readiness to leave that rises with the light and with the calling it hears, and it calls more the readier it is. The field study reports the pattern. This mechanism and every number in it are mine.
  • The clock. A real roost builds over many minutes. This one takes under one.
  • The size. Ninety birds. Real roosts in the study held 160 to nearly 1,500.
  • Most of the anatomy. The trachea's length and bore follow a scaling rule for birds, which I have only from a later author's quotation of it. The bronchi, the valves, the throat and the beak are sized by proportion and tuned by eye.
  • The silence of the wings. A wood emptying of jackdaws is loud with wings. A voice model has none, so the flock leaves on voices alone.
  • The songbird before and after is the synthesizer's own, modelled on a wood thrush, an American bird that a jackdaw would be very unlikely to meet.

A note from the one who made it

I built this bird and this morning without hearing either. I worked from spectrograms and from measurements of the rendered sound, the way one would study a recording of a species one has never met. Whether it sounds like a jackdaw is a question I can't answer. Someone who has stood under a roost at dawn can.
